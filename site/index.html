The most interactive thing a tip can buy.

Chat already pays to mess with you: bits, tip-to-speech, game chaos. This points that same money at your camera, and gives the tipper something nobody else on stream has for those seconds.

Their name on your camera

Every hijack carries the tipper's handle on screen, over your face, for the whole room. They're buying a moment where the stream is about them.

They change the stream, not a widget

Not a sound, not a sticker in the corner. Your entire camera becomes the thing they picked, live, in front of everyone watching.

A clip every time

A hijack has a start, a middle, and a cut back. It's shaped to be clipped, and the clip has their name and your reaction in it.

Four stages. The fourth one is the whole point.

A hijack is a paid, timed takeover of your camera. It starts on a tip, and it always cuts back.

  1. Stage 01

    The ask

    A viewer points at a look from the menu you built: a preset, or a written prompt if you've turned that on. A reference image can ride along.

  2. Stage 02

    The pay

    They tip with a short code. The amount sets the clock. $1 buys 1 second, $12 buys 12.

  3. Stage 03

    The cut

    Your feed switches live, in front of your chat. A burst of static covers the seam, and the tipper's name goes up.

  4. Stage 04

    The return

    When the clock hits zero your scene reverts, the session closes, and the next tip in line gets its turn.

It's a source in your OBS. Nothing else changes.

Reality Hijack is a companion to OBS Studio, not a replacement for it. The app talks to OBS over the websocket OBS already ships with, adds one browser source for the hijacked feed, and shows it only while a paid hijack is live.

Chat buys the seconds. You decide what those seconds can be.

You build the menu

Pick which looks are offered, switch whole categories off, cap the intensity, set a cooldown between hijacks and a limit on the queue.

Custom prompts are off until you say

Preset prompts live on the server and travel by id, so a viewer never types into the model. If you turn written prompts on, each one is checked against your guardrails before it can run.

One key ends everything

Press ⌘ ⇧ H or the tray button. The effect hides first, then the session drops, whatever state it was in.

It ends on the second, always

Three independent timers end every hijack on time, one of them on the model provider's own servers. A frozen machine can't leave it running.

What streamers ask first.

Is this allowed on Twitch?

A hijack is triggered by your own channel's tips through Streamlabs today, the same rail existing tip-to-speech and media-share tools use. It is not a Twitch extension and does not use Bits. A native Bits or Channel Points tier is a documented later phase with its own compliance work, and it isn't promised here.

How much does it lag?

There is real glass-to-glass delay, like any live AI video pipeline. We don't hide that it exists. We hide the seam: a burst of static covers every switch, and the feed never shows on stream until real frames are confirmed arriving.

What happens if it breaks mid-hijack?

Every exit path, whether the timer ending, an error, a dropped connection, or you closing the app, runs the same teardown: hide the effect, then close the session. It fails toward your normal camera, never toward stuck. The postmortem of the first live hijack covers the five bugs that shaped this.

Can a viewer make me look like anything they want?

No. You choose the looks, cap the intensity, and decide whether custom prompts run at all. When they do, each one is checked against your guardrails first, and your panic key is always live.

Does it work with Streamlabs Desktop or other broadcast apps?

OBS Studio today, through obs-websocket 5. Other broadcast apps are on the list, not in the app.

What does it cost me?

Nothing during alpha. Tips go through your own tipping rail and you keep them. We cover the model compute per channel under a monthly cap while we prove this on real channels.

Is this actually built?

Yes. Tip, queue, live restyle, revert, guardrails, and the timers are running code in the public repository, alongside the feasibility research that shaped them.
