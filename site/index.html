Four steps, and the fourth one matters most.

A hijack is a paid, timed takeover. It always ends, and it always ends clean.

  1. A viewer picks a look, or writes one

    From the streamer's own menu of presets, or a custom prompt if the streamer allows it. An optional reference image can ride along.

  2. They tip with a short claim code

    The code pairs the tip with the request. The amount sets the duration: $1 is 1 second, $12 is 12 seconds.

  3. The stream is hijacked, live

    The webcam feed runs through the model and takes over the scene in OBS. A short wipe covers the switch, and the tipper's name sits on screen.

  4. It snaps back, on the second

    When the paid seconds are up the scene reverts, the AI session closes, and the next tip in the queue gets its turn.

Compute at the model's published rate of $0.02 per second of realtime video. Tips flow through the streamer's own tipping rail, so Reality Hijack never touches them.

Realtime AI video is cheap enough to be interactive, and too expensive to leave running. At about $72 an hour, nobody would keep a filter on as decoration.

So the model never runs on the streamer's dime or on a timer. Every hijack is triggered and paid for by a viewer, billed by the second, and lasts only as long as the payment bought. Compute scales with revenue, not with airtime.

That inversion is the whole product. Viewers already pay to affect streamers, through bits, tip-to-speech and game-chaos tools. This points the same loop at a surface those tools can't reach: the camera.

A stuck session can never turn into a stuck bill.

The model bills by the second, so overrun is bounded three independent ways. Each cap works even if the two before it fail.

  1. on the streamer's machine

    The countdown

    The job's own timer ends the effect on the second the tip paid for, hides the source in OBS, then closes the AI session. In that order.

  2. on the streamer's machine, independently

    The watchdog

    A second timer force-ends the session ten seconds after the paid duration if the first one ever fails to fire.

  3. on the model provider's servers

    The token itself

    Every session is opened with a short-lived token that carries a hard maximum duration. If the whole computer freezes, the provider ends the session anyway.

Untrusted text never runs unchecked

Preset prompts live on the server and travel by id, so a viewer never types into the model. Custom prompts are off by default; when a streamer turns them on, every one is checked against that streamer's guardrails before it can run.

One key ends everything

Press ⌘ ⇧ H or the tray button. The effect hides first, then the session drops, whatever state it was in.

Five steps. No config files.

Reality Hijack is a companion to OBS, not a replacement. The app talks to OBS over its built-in websocket, adds a browser source for the AI feed, and keeps it hidden until real frames are flowing.

In cloud mode the model key never touches the streamer's disk. The desktop app only handles OBS, the camera, and the local video loop; the money logic runs on our side.

Questions streamers ask first.

Is this allowed on Twitch?

Today a hijack is triggered by the channel's own tips through Streamlabs, the same rail that existing tip-to-speech and media-share tools use. It is not a Twitch extension and does not use Bits. A native Bits or Channel Points tier is a documented later phase with its own compliance work, and we won't claim it until it exists.

How much lag is there?

Any realtime AI video pipeline has real glass-to-glass latency. Reality Hijack hides it rather than pretending it isn't there: a short wipe covers every switch, and OBS is only shown the AI feed after real decoded frames have arrived. A lag spike never reads as a black screen.

What happens if it breaks in the middle of a hijack?

Every exit path, whether the timer ending, an error, a dropped connection, or the app closing, runs the same teardown: hide the effect in OBS, then close the AI session. It fails toward your normal camera, never toward stuck. The postmortem of the first live hijack covers the five bugs that shaped this.

Can a viewer make me look like anything they want?

No. You choose which looks and categories are offered, set an intensity ceiling, and decide whether custom prompts are allowed at all. When they are, each one is checked against your guardrails before it can run, and you always have the panic hotkey.

Do I need a new broadcast setup?

No. It drives your existing OBS through the websocket OBS already ships with, and adds one browser source that it manages itself.

What does it cost me?

Nothing during alpha. Tips go through your own tipping rail and you keep all of them. We cover the model compute per channel under a monthly cap while we validate the product on real channels.

Is this actually built?

Yes. Tip, queue, live restyle, revert, guardrails, and the three cost caps are all running code in the public repository, alongside the feasibility research that shaped it. The demo above is a simulation of the loop; recorded clips of the real model output replace it as they're captured.
